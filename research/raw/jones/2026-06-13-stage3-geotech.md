# Jones County, NC — Stage 3 (Validate): GEOLOGICAL / GEOTECHNICAL

**Date:** 2026-06-13
**Mode:** B (DRAFT — everything tagged 🔍, nothing fabricated; site-specific items flagged HUMAN GAP)
**Stage-3 question:** Are there physical GROUND constraints — soils/bearing, flooding, storm surge, sea-level rise, seismicity — that make building a master-planned new city in Jones County, NC a cost problem or an outright kill? Does a buildable, dry, firm, flood-safe upland bench exist *at scale*?
**Demand frame (placeholder spec, do not re-derive):**
- Build-out ~50,000 people on **15,000–50,000 acres** (~23–78 sq mi), residential + light industrial.
- County context: Jones is ~471 sq mi (~302,000 ac), pop ~9,172, lower NC Coastal Plain. ~75%+ of land is wetlands / pocosin / Croatan NF / swamp. Trent River bisects the county through Trenton (seat) and Pollocksville; Neuse basin to the north.
- Realized hazard: Hurricane Florence (Sept 2018) put ~half of Pollocksville and much of Trenton underwater — record flood.

---

## TL;DR

🔍 **Overall: CONCERN bordering on KILL-by-squeeze — the single hardest physical screen of the three stage-3 geotech-class issues.** The hazards here are not independent; they **overlap and compound**. The same flat, low (~25–50 ft), high-water-table terrain that is wetland/pocosin is *also* the terrain that floods, and the firmer sandy uplands are interspersed in narrow bands between the wet ground. There is no evidence of a single contiguous 15,000–50,000-acre dry, firm, flood-safe bench.

🔍 **Soils/bearing: CONCERN→FAIL on the wet fraction; routine on the sand ridges.** NC has >1 million acres of organic (peat/muck) soils concentrated in the lower Coastal Plain, and Jones is squarely in that belt (pocosins = peat). Organic/peat soils have near-zero bearing capacity, high compressibility, and large long-term settlement — they are an *avoid* category, not a *treat* category, for buildings at scale. The buildable firm material is the **interfluve sand ridges / upland flats** (the kind of ground Trenton/Comfort sit on). The screen is therefore about how much *firm, well-drained, non-flooding* upland exists contiguously — and that is exactly the resource the wetland+flood overlap eats into.

🔍 **Flooding: CONCERN→FAIL near rivers; documented, realized hazard.** Florence 2018 was not a model — it happened: Trenton's courthouse, post office, two schools flooded; ~half of Pollocksville underwater for days; 572+ homes damaged countywide; county manager said Florence ran 4–5 ft higher than Floyd (1999), which had itself been called a 500-year flood. On flat terrain the problem is *also* pluvial/poor-drainage (sheet flooding from extreme rain that can't run off), not just riverine — this hits even ground that isn't in a mapped SFHA.

🔍 **Storm surge: CONCERN — surge reaches FAR inland up the Trent/Neuse.** This is the surprising one. Surge from Florence traveled ~40 mi up the Neuse to New Bern (10.1 ft) and up the Trent to **Pollocksville (~9–10 ft surge), which is ~50 river-miles from the open coast.** So "far enough inland to dodge surge" is NOT automatically true along the river corridors. A build site set back from the Trent/Neuse tidal reaches and on higher upland is needed to escape surge — which again pushes siting onto the limited upland.

🔍 **Sea-level rise: COST/CONCERN, indirect.** NC CRC Science Panel / 2024 SLR update: ~1.1 ft by mid-century, ~3.8 ft (medium) to up to ~7 ft (high) by 2100; CRC planning value historically ~1 m (~39 in) by 2100. Trenton sits ~26–33 ft; SLR does not directly inundate the plausible upland build elevations this century. BUT SLR raises the tidal base, pushes the salt wedge and surge ceiling upriver, and worsens drainage/backwater on already-flat land — it makes flooding worse over the project's life rather than drowning the bench outright.

🔍 **Seismicity: ROUTINE (cost-neutral).** NC Coastal Plain is low seismic hazard; design proceeds under routine IBC seismic categories (Site Class D assumption; soft/organic soils can bump site class locally but that is handled per-site). Not a driver.

🔍 **NET:** A new city here is buildable *in principle* only on the firm upland fraction, and only with the build footprint deliberately threaded onto sand ridges set back and elevated above the Trent/Neuse flood/surge corridors. Whether 15,000–50,000 contiguous acres of such ground exist is the live question and is **site-specific (HUMAN GAP)** — but the prior on a ~75%-wet county with a realized record flood is *unfavorable*. The hazards jointly squeeze the buildable area; this reads as **CONCERN with real KILL risk at the upper (50,000-ac) footprint.**

## 1. SOILS / BEARING — ORGANIC (POCOSIN/PEAT) vs. FIRM SAND

🔍 **The two-population problem.** Lower-Coastal-Plain Jones County ground splits into two very different geotechnical populations:

- **Organic / peat / muck soils (pocosins, swamp, bottomland):** NC has **>1 million acres of organic soils**, overwhelmingly in the tidewater/lower Coastal Plain — exactly Jones's setting. Pocosins are *peatlands*: deep, acidic, carbon-rich peat accumulated over millennia. Geotechnically these are the worst common foundation material: very low bearing capacity, very high compressibility, large and long-duration consolidation settlement, low shear strength, and high/perched water table. For buildings at scale these are an **AVOID** category. "Treatment" (excavate-and-replace, surcharge/preload, deep foundations, geopiers, wick drains) is possible but expensive and, over thousands of acres, uneconomic — the correct move is to *not build on them*. Sources: NC State Climate Office "Our Curious Coast: Soils and Agriculture"; NCpedia/NHP pocosins; carolinawetlands.org pocosins.
- **Firm sandy uplands / interfluve flats:** The non-wet fraction — the sand ridges and upland flats where Trenton (el. ~26–33 ft) and Comfort (~52 ft) sit — is normal, buildable Coastal-Plain ground (sands/sandy loams over coastal-plain sediments). Spread footings / slabs feasible; routine. This is the **only** material a 50k-person city should sit on.

🔍 **High water table is pervasive** even on the better ground — flat, low terrain in a wet climate keeps the water table shallow. Implications: basements impractical, slab-on-grade with engineered fill and underdrains, stormwater infiltration limited (works against low-impact drainage design), and dewatering needed for any deep utilities. This is a **COST**, not a kill, on the upland; it is part of why the wet fraction is a *kill*.

🔍 **The screen is a MAP problem, not a soils-chemistry problem.** Whether the city pencils depends on the spatial extent and contiguity of firm upland vs. organic ground inside any candidate AOI. **HUMAN GAP:** pull SSURGO/gSSURGO for the specific AOI (NRCS Web Soil Survey) and classify by drainage class + organic content (histosols/mucky modifiers) and depth to water table. Until then, the county-scale prior (~75% wet) governs: firm upland is the *minority* land cover.

## 2. FLOODING — FEMA ZONES, FLORENCE REALIZED EXTENT, PLUVIAL

🔍 **Realized, not modeled.** Hurricane Florence (Sept 14–17, 2018):
- **Trenton (seat):** courthouse, post office, two schools, and numerous homes/businesses flooded.
- **Pollocksville:** ~half the town's homes/businesses underwater for several days; Main Street under ~20 ft of water at peak.
- **Countywide:** ≥572 homes, 43 businesses, 22 public/nonprofit buildings damaged; >$19M damage; >750 families lost "everything." County manager: Florence ran **4–5 ft higher than Hurricane Floyd (1999)**, which had been called a 500-year flood. Pollocksville saw a 9-ft surge in day one *then* 25–30 in of rain.
- Sources: NC Health News (2018); Public Radio East (2018); PBS NewsHour (2018); The Assembly NC (Pollocksville).

🔍 **Riverine:** The Trent River and tributaries (Jumping Creek, Crooked Run, Long Branch, French Branch, etc.) thread the county; the Neuse bounds the north. FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (1% / 100-yr and 0.2% / 500-yr) follow these corridors and, on flat terrain, spread *wide* — shallow gradients mean a small stage rise inundates large lateral area.

🔍 **Pluvial / poor-drainage:** On nearly-flat ground (county relief is tens of feet, not hundreds), extreme rainfall ponds and sheet-floods because it cannot drain — this floods ground that is *not* in a mapped SFHA. Florence's 25–30 in of rain is the archetype. This is a structural disadvantage of the whole flat county, partly independent of river proximity.

🔍 **How much of the county is in/near floodplain — HUMAN GAP (quantify at AOI).** A specific SFHA % was not retrievable via search. Pull the % of any candidate AOI in Zone AE/A/X-shaded from the **NC Floodplain Mapping Program (FRIS / flood.nc.gov)** and **FEMA NFHL / MSC**. Q3 data exists but is outdated — use the NC effective layer (NC OneMap "NC Effective Flood Zones"). Prior expectation given ~75% wet + riverine network: a *large* share is in or adjacent to floodplain, and the dry-upland residual is fragmented.

## 3. STORM SURGE / HURRICANES

🔍 **Surge penetrates much farther inland than intuition suggests — via the rivers.** Florence drove surge ~40 mi up the Neuse to **New Bern (10.1 ft above ground)** and up the **Trent to Pollocksville (~9–10 ft), ~50 river-miles from the open coast.** So a site can be tens of miles inland and *still* be in the surge envelope if it sits in the Trent/Neuse tidal/estuarine corridor. NHC/USGS gauges captured surge ~40 mi up the Neuse. Sources: iWeatherNet (New Bern surge); Weather Underground (Florence surge records); NHC storm surge overview; The Assembly NC (Pollocksville ~50 mi from coast).

🔍 **Implication for siting:** the build footprint must be **set back from the Trent/Neuse tidal reaches and on higher upland** to clear surge — which is consistent with (and reinforces) the soils screen: build on the upland sand benches, away from the river corridors. Upstream of the tidal limit (Trent above Trenton trends fresher/riverine), surge gives way to riverine/pluvial flooding — still a hazard, but not salt surge.

🔍 **Extreme rainfall is unavoidable** anywhere in eastern NC — the city *will* be exposed to tropical-rainfall events (Florence, Floyd, Matthew, Dorian) regardless of siting. That argues for generous stormwater conveyance/retention, elevated finished floors, and avoiding the wet/low ground entirely. This is a **COST** layer applied to the whole project.

## 4. SEA-LEVEL RISE

🔍 **Projections (NC).** NC CRC Science Panel / 2024 NC SLR Science Update and NC Sea Grant: ~**1.1 ft by ~2050**; medium ~**3.8 ft by 2100**, with intermediate-to-high scenarios spanning ~**2–7 ft by 2100**; CRC historically used ~**1 m (~39 in) by 2100** as a planning value. RSLR is higher in northern NC than southern; land subsidence varies. Sources: NC Sea Grant Coastwatch (Winter 2025 SLR update); NC DEQ 2024 SLR Science Update; Climate Central NC report.

🔍 **Does it touch plausible build elevations?** Trenton ~26–33 ft; upland targets would be on similar-or-higher ground. **Direct inundation of the upland bench this century: NO.** But SLR is an *amplifier* here, not a drowner: it raises the tidal datum, lifts the surge/backwater ceiling and pushes it upriver, slows drainage on flat land, and expands future floodplains. Net effect = the **flooding problem (Sec 2–3) gets worse over the 50–100 yr life of the city.** Read: **COST/CONCERN, indirect**, layered on top of the flood screen rather than a standalone kill.

## 5. SEISMICITY

🔍 **Routine — not a driver.** The NC Coastal Plain is a **low seismic hazard** region (USGS NC seismicity map; ThinkHazard "low"; NC OSFM seismic design categories). Design proceeds under standard IBC/ASCE 7 seismic provisions assuming Site Class D unless soils dictate otherwise. Caveat: soft/organic soils (the pocosin fraction) can locally push site class toward E/F and amplify motion — another reason to build on the firm upland, but this is a per-site detail, cost-neutral at the screening level. Sources: USGS Seismicity Map of NC; NC OSFM 0301.2 Seismic Design Categories; ThinkHazard NC Earthquake.

## NET READ (cost vs kill)

🔍 **Verdict: CONCERN with material KILL risk by squeeze — the hazards are correlated and they overlap on the same ground.**

- The buildable resource is the **firm, well-drained, non-flooding upland sand fraction.** Everything else — pocosin/peat (no bearing), riverine floodplain, surge corridor, high-water-table flats — is *avoid*. These avoid-zones are not separate problems; they **co-locate** (the wet ground is the low ground is the flood ground), so they don't subtract independently — they reinforce, concentrating the city onto a smaller, more fragmented upland residual.
- **At the 15,000-ac (lower) footprint:** plausibly findable on a good upland block, set back from the Trent/Neuse — *if* SSURGO + FEMA at the AOI confirm contiguity. CONCERN, possibly pass-with-work.
- **At the 50,000-ac (upper) footprint (~78 sq mi, ~1/6 of the entire county):** very hard to believe a contiguous dry/firm/flood-safe block that large exists in a ~75%-wet county with a realized record flood and Croatan NF occupying a big share. This pushes toward **KILL** at the large footprint.
- **No single hazard is independently a clean kill** (seismicity routine; SLR doesn't drown the bench; soils are avoidable; surge dodge-able by setback). **The kill risk is the joint constraint:** does enough good ground exist, contiguously, after you remove peat + floodplain + surge corridor + federal forest? That is the decisive, site-specific question.
- This is the **harder geotech screen** relative to typical inland sites: most counties don't have a documented town-drowning flood in living memory. Jones does (2018), twice in a generation (1999, 2018).

**One-line:** Buildable only on the upland sand benches threaded between the wetlands and set back/elevated above the Trent–Neuse flood/surge corridors; whether 15k–50k *contiguous* such acres exist is the live, site-specific gate, and the county-scale prior is unfavorable — CONCERN, trending KILL at the larger footprint.

I'll treat this as an entitlement-risk framework rather than assuming a specific city. I'll anchor the answer in legal mechanisms that usually create durability, then separate cheap signals from truly binding evidence.

Assuming California-style land-use law, the honest answer is: you cannot get durable "yes" evidence before spending serious money unless the jurisdiction is willing to give you an actual vested entitlement. Everything short of that is just willingness evidence.

The cheapest real evidence is not a mayor meeting, staff memo, LOI, or concept approval. It is a public, legally operative act that either vests rights or proves the jurisdiction can lawfully enter the vesting path.

## Best target: a Development Agreement + vesting map, after challenge windows

For a mega-project, the cleanest durable package is usually:

1. **Certified environmental review / valid CEQA path.** Then file the notice that starts the short challenge period. In California, CEQA challenges are usually cut off after 30 days for a Notice of Determination, 35 days for a Notice of Exemption, or 180 days if the notice path is not used.
2. **Development Agreement approved by ordinance.** A California development agreement can remain enforceable even if the city later changes the general plan, specific plan, zoning, subdivision rules, or building regulations. That is the core "survive political turnover" instrument.
3. **Referendum period survived.** A development agreement is a legislative act approved by ordinance and is subject to referendum. In California cities, a referendum petition against an ordinance generally must be submitted within 30 days of attestation and meet signature thresholds. So the observable evidence improves dramatically once that window closes without a qualified referendum, or if a referendum is held and the approval survives.
4. **Vesting Tentative Map, if subdivision is involved.** A vesting tentative map gives a statutory vested right to proceed in substantial compliance with the applicable ordinances, policies, and standards. California's statute is explicit that the private sector should be able to rely on the approved vesting map before spending major resources, without later local action frustrating the project.

That is the first package that looks like: "This jurisdiction can grant yes, and the yes is legally sticky."

## What is the cheapest observable evidence?

The cheapest meaningful test is a sequenced public vote that forces the jurisdiction to expose its legal theory. The evidence ladder:

| Evidence | Cost | What it proves | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Staff enthusiasm, mayor meeting, economic-development letter | Low | Interest | Almost nothing legally durable |
| City attorney memo saying the jurisdiction has authority to process a DA / specific plan / zoning amendment of this scale | Low-medium | Legal pathway exists | Political will |
| Public council study session with direction to negotiate a DA and process entitlements | Low-medium | Political willingness under public scrutiny | Durability |
| Council-approved term sheet / ENA / reimbursement agreement for DA + CEQA + specific plan | Medium | They are willing to spend institutional capital | Still not an entitlement |
| Specific plan / zoning / general plan amendment adopted, CEQA notice filed, challenge period expires | High | They can approve the magnitude | Still may not fully vest |
| Development Agreement adopted by ordinance, referendum period survived, recorded | High | Durable yes | Still subject to state/federal law, health/safety exceptions, litigation risk |
| DA + vesting tentative map + CEQA period expired + no active referendum/litigation | Highest pre-construction signal | Best pre-spend durability | Not absolute immunity |

So the cheapest observable evidence worth paying for is probably: a publicly adopted council resolution directing staff and counsel to negotiate a development agreement for the full project envelope, paired with a written city-attorney opinion that the jurisdiction has legal authority to approve and vest that envelope, plus a cost-reimbursement / processing agreement.

But the cheapest evidence that the jurisdiction can grant and *keep* a yes is stronger: a development agreement ordinance and, where applicable, a vesting tentative map, with CEQA and referendum challenge windows cleared.

## Ballot initiative nuance

Do not rely on a friendly initiative as the vesting mechanism. California courts have held that a statutory development agreement cannot be adopted by initiative; it has to be negotiated between the local government and the property owner and approved through the statutory process.

Voters can still attack legislative approvals through referendum, and later initiatives can change general law. The reason the development agreement matters is that it functions like a contract vesting project rules against later local changes. But it is not magic: it can still be vulnerable if the original approval is defective, CEQA is mishandled, the city lacked authority, the DA is inconsistent with the general/specific plan, or later state/federal law requires changes.

## Practical answer

Look for jurisdictions where the path can be: specific plan / zoning envelope → CEQA clearance → development agreement → vesting tentative map → recorded rights → challenge periods expired.

The cheapest "go/no-go" evidence is not verbal support. It is a publicly adopted, lawyer-signed, procedurally correct first step that commits the jurisdiction to the exact vesting path for the full 10–100x project envelope.

The first evidence I would truly underwrite is: **recorded DA + vesting map + expired CEQA/referendum windows.** Anything before that is a signal. Useful, but not durable.

# Raw output — Agent 2 (regime-first analysis)

> Prompt: the core question from [framework/00](../../../framework/00-the-question.md), posed with no project context. Preserved verbatim for traceability; claims unverified (note: some cited sources are non-authoritative).

Short answer to "where": not by finding a willing jurisdiction, but by finding a state legal regime where the durable instrument vests early, vests as a property or contract interest rather than a legislative one, and sits outside the reach of both a mini-NEPA litigation veto and local referendum. In the U.S. that points hard at the Texas-class states and away from your home state. Offshore-sovereign and federal-land routes both fail the durability test in instructive ways. Let me take the two questions in the order your funnel demands — regime first, then the cheap screen.

## Where the durable-cheap yes actually lives

Durability against turnover + organized opposition + ballot all reduce to one legal question: at what point, and in what form, does your entitlement become something a later hostile actor cannot strip without either paying for it or breaching a contract? That has four sub-variables, all readable from statute before a dollar moves:

1. **Vesting timing.** Most states follow the majority "reliance" rule — rights vest only after a permit issues and you've sunk substantial construction money. That is durability bought late and expensive. A minority of states vest early and cheaply: Washington vests the regulatory regime at the moment of a complete application, before the public has weighed in, and Texas's Chapter 245 permit-vesting statute freezes the rules in effect at the original application across the whole project. The minority rule favors developers because it provides certainty well before the project has begun, or before the public is given a chance to voice opposition. In some regimes the vested right, once established, effectively lasts until the agreement expires. Early-vesting states let you buy durability at the $2M stage rather than the $40M stage — exactly the spend-stage axis you flagged.
2. **Whether your durability instrument is itself referendum-exposed.** This is the trap California sets. A development agreement there is the strongest instrument available — and development agreements are legislative acts and therefore subject to referendum, so the flexibility the tool affords is bounded by the same community veto you were trying to escape. You can manufacture durability and have voters strike the instrument that manufactures it. The states you want are ones where land-use entitlements are quasi-judicial (not subject to referendum) or where the DA is treated as a binding contract protected by the Contracts Clause rather than as repealable legislation.
3. **Mini-NEPA with a private right of action.** CEQA is the standing litigation veto, and it's potent precisely because any opponent can sue at the project level. Only about a third of states have a "little NEPA," and most lack CEQA's private enforcement breadth. Texas, most of the Mountain West and the South have none. A state with no mini-NEPA private right of action removes an entire category of veto holder for $0.
4. **Boundary gatekeeper.** California's LAFCO is the thing California Forever could not route around. Many states annex by municipal ordinance with no boundary commission — one fewer veto holder.

Texas is the near-best-case onshore intersection of all four: early statutory vesting, no CEQA-equivalent private litigation veto, special-district tooling (MUDs and legislatively created districts) that can pre-empt local control, annexation/ETJ regimes friendlier than LAFCO, and weak local referendum reach over land-use legislative acts. Utah and Arizona are second-tier (Arizona's Prop 207 regulatory-takings statute is actually a durability asset — it makes a later downzoning compensable). The standing caution is the Reedy Creek/Disney episode: a legislatively created special district is durable against local veto but exposed to state turnover. Special legislation that lives at legislative pleasure is not durable; the version that survives is the one whose revocation triggers a compensable taking or a contract claim — because by then reliance has vested.

Two non-obvious answers to "where." First, **buy land that is already entitled at scale** rather than manufacturing entitlements — defunct master-planned communities with live DAs, base-reuse authorities, large vested industrial parcels. That is the only place a cheap durable yes at this magnitude reliably already exists, because the durability was purchased by someone else and (if vested) survives the sale. Second, the routes that look like shortcuts are dead ends: the federal "Freedom Cities" path is, as of early 2026, stalled — no sites selected, no land dispositions, no enabling legislation enacted, and an executive-order-only version is the least durable option, revocable by the next president and unable to override statutes like the Clean Water Act or Endangered Species Act, which only Congress can change. The offshore-sovereign route is the cautionary tale that should reframe your whole durability concept: Próspera held a 50-year legal-stability guarantee embedded in the ZEDE framework — and that framework was repealed in 2022 anyway, with the Supreme Court declaring it unconstitutional retroactively. Their remedy now is a $10.7 billion ICSID arbitration with no near-term ruling. A sovereign stability promise converted "durable entitlement" into "spend the money, then litigate a decade for damages, not the project." That is the precise failure mode you're trying to avoid.

## The cheapest observable evidence that a jurisdiction can grant — and keep — a yes

Ordered by expected eliminations × dollars saved ÷ cost, killers first. The first three are $0, read from statute and constitution:

1. Does the state have a mini-NEPA with a project-level private right of action? Yes → structural litigation veto present → near-disqualify for "cheap durable."
2. Does the state constitution extend both initiative and referendum to local legislative land-use acts, and is a DA classified as such a legislative act? Yes → the ballot can reach your durability instrument itself → near-disqualify.
3. Vesting rule: early-statutory or majority-reliance? Majority → durability is only purchasable at construction-stage spend.

Then the cheap base-rate observables (days of public-records work): has any GP-amendment-magnitude project ever been put to or killed by referendum in this specific jurisdiction (pull the election history); the mini-NEPA litigation injunction rate for comparable projects (court dockets); governing-board flip frequency and margin (a 3-2 board with an election in 18 months is not durability); and the presence of a standing slow-growth/land-trust litigant with a win record — the Solano Together/Greenbelt Alliance pattern that organized against California Forever is exactly the $0-detectable signal.

The single most decisive cheap screen is the conjunction of (1)+(2): a state with both a private-right mini-NEPA and referendum reach over land-use legislation can kill your project through two independent channels that require no one to be unwilling. That combination is structurally California, and it's identifiable before any spend — which means the funnel's highest-value first output may be eliminating your home state at this magnitude.

The deepest "can it keep it" observable, though, is one statute-reading question: does the durable instrument vest a property or contract interest (compensable or specifically enforceable to revoke) or merely a legislative one (revocable at pleasure)? Próspera proves the surface signal — "is there a stability guarantee" — is worthless. The real test is what claim accrues on breach and whether that claim yields specific performance/injunction or only money. Damages-only durability means you can be removed; you just get paid. If you want the city to exist rather than a settlement, you need an instrument that vests before opposition organizes and whose breach is enjoinable.

On the closing scoping question — this is load-bearing and the California Forever pivot answers it empirically. They abandoned the county-GP-amendment-by-ballot route and switched to annexation through Suisun City, which moves the dominant veto from county-electorate-referendum to municipal council + LAFCO, with the annexation potentially sent to the ballot if there is enough opposition and likely lawsuits from opponents at the end regardless. The lesson: incorporate-new-city and entitle-at-scale aren't just different veto stacks — the path is itself a choice variable the engine should optimize over, not take as given.

Two honest caveats. I'm not a lawyer, and the precise contours of vesting, referendum reach, and DA classification are state-specific and occasionally county-specific — local land-use counsel is the source of truth; the value here is the screen, not the legal opinion. And the base-rate honesty check stands: it is entirely possible the cheap durable yes at 10–100x magnitude doesn't exist anywhere at this scale, because magnitude itself summons the veto mechanisms. If that's the finding, the elimination funnel did its job — and "buy already-entitled at scale" becomes the only live option rather than a fallback.

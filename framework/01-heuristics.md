# 01 — Golden Heuristics

The rules that order all other work. Each exists because it's cheap to apply and expensive to violate.

## 1. Kill-rate ordering — region-conditional

Evaluate candidates by vulnerability, not preference. Run the layer with the highest kill-rate first, and don't proceed until the current layer clears. **The kill order depends on region:**

| Region | Layer 1 (kill first) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Intermountain West (WY, SD, NV, UT) | Water rights / basin appropriation | Prior appropriation doctrine; closed basins are absolute kills |
| Southeast (NC, etc.) | Wetlands, floodplain, sewer capacity, ETJ/annexation exposure | Riparian doctrine makes water rarely binding; §404 wetlands and municipal ETJ are |

The cardinal rule survives either way: *fall in love at the infrastructure layer, but kill the project at the constraint layer.*

## 2. The infrastructure multiplier

Land cost (L) and offsite infrastructure cost (I) are a single, inseparable variable:

```
Total Acquisition Cost = L + I
```

Cheap land is usually cheap because someone else already paid to prove it's hard to serve. $500/acre land needing a 40-mile water pipeline and a new substation is more expensive than $5,000/acre land beside an existing high-voltage corridor and water trunk line.

## 3. The scale-threshold heuristic

Every jurisdiction has a project scale at which the political equilibrium flips from "pro-growth" to "anti-colonization." Estimate it before buying: *what is the largest private development this county has absorbed in the last 20 years?* Enter below that threshold and grow incrementally. Adding 50,000 people to a county of 500 is not a development; it's an ecological shift.

Note this heuristic is reinforced by the durability analysis ([00](00-the-question.md)): magnitude itself summons veto mechanisms. Entering below threshold may be not just politically but *legally* the cheapest durability strategy — smaller approvals attract fewer referendum drives and lawsuits, and vest before opposition organizes.

## 4. The optionality preservation rule

Never let the search collapse to a single county until purchase options or LOIs are secured in at least two, ideally three, distinct jurisdictions. Negotiating leverage with commissioners, utilities, and landowners evaporates the moment they realize they're the only option. *Negotiate with all, commit to none, until water, zoning path, and infrastructure geometry are confirmed in writing.*

## 5. County-level vs. site-level signals (don't mis-level kills)

Floodplain, conservation easements, slope, and soils are *site*-level facts — a county with 400,000 acres is not killed by a floodplain. Governance signals (vesting law, board composition, planning capacity) are *county*- or *state*-level. The funnel screens jurisdictions on governance signals and uses "best available site in county" proxies for physical ones; site-level diligence belongs to late stages only.

## 6. Disclosure is a strategic variable, not a default

Stealth land assembly *caused* the backlash California Forever's scale-threshold problem predicted. The choice of when and how to go public is part of the entitlement strategy and must be decided per jurisdiction, not assumed. The relevant tradeoff: stealth lowers land prices but converts the eventual reveal into evidence of bad faith, which organized opposition will use forever.

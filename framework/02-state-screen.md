# 02 — The State Screen

**The state is a more powerful screening unit than the county.** Water doctrine, vesting law, referendum reach, environmental review statutes, and annexation regimes are all set at the state level — and they kill or rescue entire states in an afternoon of statute-reading, before any county work begins. Counties compete on willingness; states determine whether a durable yes is *possible*.

All claims produced by this screen are **hypotheses for local land-use counsel to confirm**, not legal conclusions. The screen's job is to generate the right questions cheaply.

## The four statute-readable variables

All four are readable for ~$0 from statute, constitution, and case law summaries.

### 1. Vesting timing

*When do development rights become un-strippable, and at what spend stage?*

- **Early-statutory states** (minority rule): rights vest at application. Texas Ch. 245 freezes the rules in effect at original application across the whole project; Washington vests the regulatory regime at complete application. Durability is purchasable at the ~$2M stage.
- **Reliance-rule states** (majority): rights vest only after a permit issues *and* substantial construction money is sunk. Durability arrives at the ~$40M stage — after opposition has had years to organize.

### 2. Referendum reach over the durability instrument

*Can voters strike the instrument that creates durability?*

The California trap: a development agreement is the strongest available instrument there, but it's classified as a legislative act and therefore subject to referendum — the ballot can reach the very thing you built to escape the ballot. Prefer states where large entitlements are quasi-judicial (not referendum-eligible) or where the DA is treated as a contract protected by the Contracts Clause rather than repealable legislation.

### 3. Mini-NEPA with a private right of action

*Does any opponent have a standing project-level litigation veto?*

Only about a third of states have a "little NEPA," and most lack CEQA's private-enforcement breadth. A state with no mini-NEPA private right of action removes an entire category of veto holder for $0. Texas, most of the Mountain West, and most of the South have none.

### 4. Boundary gatekeeper

*Who controls annexation and incorporation?*

California's LAFCO is the veto California Forever could not route around. Many states annex by municipal ordinance with no boundary commission — one fewer veto holder. Check: boundary commission existence, incorporation petition requirements, ETJ regimes (NC municipalities hold extraterritorial jurisdiction; this is a county-screen landmine set at the state level).

## The breach test (applied per instrument)

For each candidate durability instrument in the state (DA, vested map, special district charter, annexation agreement):

> On revocation, what claim accrues — and is the remedy an injunction/specific performance, or damages only?

Damages-only means removable-but-compensated. See [00 — The Question](00-the-question.md#the-breach-test).

A special-district caution: a legislatively created district (the Reedy Creek/Disney pattern) is durable against *local* veto but exposed to *state* turnover. The version that survives is one whose revocation triggers a compensable taking or contract claim.

## The decisive conjunction

The single most powerful cheap kill: **(variable 3) + (variable 2)**. A state with both a private-right mini-NEPA *and* referendum reach over land-use legislation can kill the project through two independent channels that require no individual to be unwilling. That combination is structurally California — and it's identifiable before any spend.

## Screen output

One page per state ([template](../results/states/_template.md)) covering: the four variables with citations, breach test per instrument, water doctrine, the available [entitlement paths](05-entitlement-paths.md) and each path's veto stack, and a verdict — *what is the strongest durable instrument obtainable in this state, at what spend stage, and what can still kill it?*

Reference points from prior analysis (unverified): Texas as near-best onshore intersection of all four variables (Ch. 245 vesting, no mini-NEPA, MUD/special-district tooling, friendly annexation); Utah and Arizona second tier (AZ Prop 207 makes later downzoning compensable — a durability asset). **None of the current pilot states (WY, SD, NC) have been screened yet.**
